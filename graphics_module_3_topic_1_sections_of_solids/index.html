Textbook problem

    Step 1 of 3

    Step one

    Solid

    Every solid starts in its textbook default pose, standing on the HP. Size and pose controls arrive with the projection pass.

    Section plane

    The tinted sheet is the cutting plane. The part of the solid on the far side of it — above the plane, or toward you — is removed.

    The section

    The crimson face is the section — the flat figure where the plane passes through the solid. Orbit the view until you look at that face square-on: what you see then is its true shape, the figure a sectional drawing must show.

    Try cutting the cone at different inclinations — the section walks through the classic curves: circle, ellipse, parabola, hyperbola.

    Orthographic views

    The solid now projects onto the three reference planes: the top view (teal, on the floor), the front view (amber, on the back wall) and the side view (violet, on the profile wall). Solid lines are edges the observer of that view can see; dashed lines are edges the body hides.

    The cut surface is hatched at 45° wherever it shows as an area. That hatched figure is the apparent shape — foreshortened, because the section is inclined to the plane it is projected on. In the view the cutting plane is edge-on to, the section collapses to a line.

    True shape

    The floating sheet is an auxiliary view: a drawing plane held parallel to the cutting plane itself. On it the section appears at full size — the true shape — with none of the foreshortening the ordinary views suffer.

    Compare it with the hatched apparent shape in the views — same figure, different sizes. Only the auxiliary view reports the section’s real measurements.
