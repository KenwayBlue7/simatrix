Choose a Solid

The solid

Drag the viewport to turn it. Look at where its faces, edges and curves are — those are what the construction will have to place.

Every solid here is constructed by the same four phases, in the same order. What changes between them is only the third phase, and only because the shapes themselves differ.

Dimensions

These are the only measurements this solid needs. Change one and watch the model, its labels and its overall size all follow.

Overall size

These three numbers — the overall width, depth and height — are what the enclosing box is built from in Step 4. Notice they are not always the dimensions you typed: a pentagon's width across its corners is not its side length.

Three views, one solid

On the left of the viewport is a real drawing of the solid: the front view above the XY line, the top view below it, and the side view beside the front.

Click any view — what does it show you?

Selecting a view turns the camera to look along that direction and lights the matching face on the solid. Click it again to come back to the pictorial view.

The faint grey lines running between the views are projection lines. They are what keeps the three views aligned, and they are the visible proof that these are three records of one object rather than three drawings.

These three views completely describe one solid. Everything the isometric drawing needs — every width, every depth, every height — is already on this sheet. That is why the construction reads its sizes from here and never invents them.

Four phases

Use the A · B · C · D buttons at the bottom of the viewport to advance the construction one phase at a time. Go back to any phase as often as you like — the order is the thing worth remembering.

Notice what did not change when you picked a different solid: the axes, the box, and the order. Only Phase C differs — and only because the shapes differ.

Two forms, one method

Topic 1 named the two forms of isometric drawing. Here is the whole difference between them — and it is smaller than most students expect.

Isometric Projection Isometric scale · ≈ 0.816 Every length measured along the axes is reduced to about 0.816 of its true value. This is what the object would actually project as, so the drawing is a true projection — and it is the smaller of the two on screen.
Isometric View True dimensions · 1 : 1 Lengths are set off at their true values, with no reduction. It is quicker to draw and easier to read, which is why most textbook problems ask for it.
DimensionTrue sizeDrawn · 1:1

The construction sequence is identical. Same axes, same bounding box, same Phase C, same finish — in the same order. Switch between the two above and watch what actually changes: the drawing re-scales, the numbers follow, and not one step of the method moves.

The chain

Watch the whole process once more, without stopping. Select any link in the chain at the bottom of the viewport to replay from there.

  1. Orthographic views — they describe the solid, and they carry every dimension.
  2. Axes — three directions from one corner; everything is measured along them.
  3. Bounding box — overall width, depth and height transferred onto those axes.
  4. Construction — the shape built inside the box, using the box to place it.
  5. Finished isometric — guides fade; the visible edges remain.

You are now ready to solve isometric drawing problems.
